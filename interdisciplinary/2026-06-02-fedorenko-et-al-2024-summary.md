# Fedorenko, Piantadosi & Gibson (2024) — Language is primarily a tool for communication rather than thought

**Nature 2024, Vol 630: 575–584 (Perspective)**

---

## Overview

- **Paper type**: Perspective (review + argument synthesis)
- **Central claim**: Language is primarily a tool for **communication**, not for thought. Language reflects human cognitive sophistication but does not give rise to it.
- **Number of evidence blocks**: 4
- **Evidence block purposes**:
  1. The language network in the brain — establishes the neural "target" for testing
  2. Double dissociation between language and thought — shows language is neither necessary nor sufficient for thought
  3. Language is optimized for communication — structural properties of languages are explained by communicative pressures
  4. Evolutionary evidence — brain evolution shows parallel expansion of multiple networks, not language driving everything
- **Block relationships**: Block 1 defines what "language" means neurally → Block 2 uses this target to show language and thought are separable → Block 3 provides positive evidence for the communication function → Block 4 places the argument in evolutionary context
- **Key debate**: Language-for-thought (Chomsky, Carruthers, Vygotsky, etc.) vs. Language-for-communication (Hockett, Pinker, Gibson, etc.)
- **Conclusion**: Language is doubly dissociated from thought (not necessary, not sufficient). Language shows hallmarks of an efficient communication code. Language likely transformed humanity through **cultural transmission** (cumulative knowledge), not by providing a substrate for thinking.

---

### High-Level Summary

This paper synthesizes two decades of neuroscience, linguistics, and evolutionary biology to argue that language is a communication tool, not a thinking tool. The core move is to identify the brain's "language network" as a precise neural target, then show it is silent during diverse thinking tasks and that people with severe aphasia can still think perfectly well. The paper further argues that cross-linguistic structural properties (word length, ambiguity, dependency minimization) are best explained by communicative efficiency pressures. What makes this compelling is the convergence of evidence across very different methodologies (fMRI, lesion studies, computational linguistics, cross-linguistic typology, animal cognition), all pointing to the same conclusion.

## Block 1: The Language Network in the Brain

### Claim

There is a well-defined, identifiable brain network that supports language — and this network is the correct target for testing whether language is used for thought.

### Discipline/Method

Cognitive neuroscience (fMRI, magnetoencephalography, intracranial recordings)

### Key Evidence

- The **language network** is a set of interconnected areas in the **left hemisphere** (frontal and temporal cortex), identifiable in individual brains using fMRI "localizer" paradigms
- Two key properties:
  1. **Modality-independent**: responds to spoken, written, and signed language; active during both comprehension and production → stores abstract linguistic knowledge, not just speech
  2. **Sensitive to both meaning and structure**: all areas respond to word meanings (words > nonwords) and syntactic structure (sentences > word lists)
- This corresponds to Hauser, Chomsky & Fitch's (2002) "faculty of language in the narrow sense"
- Updates the classic Broca/Wernicke model: Broca's area = articulatory planning, Wernicke's area = speech perception, but the higher-level language network is a separate distributed system (Box 2)

### Logic

If language-for-thought hypotheses are correct, this network should be active during thinking tasks. It provides a clear, falsifiable target.

### Limitations/Caveats

- The localizer paradigm defines the network functionally, which is a strength, but it means the boundary of "language" is determined by the experimental contrast used
- The paper acknowledges the classic model was not entirely wrong, just incomplete

## Block 2: Double Dissociation Between Language and Thought

### Claim

Language is **neither necessary nor sufficient** for thought. This double dissociation is the strongest evidence against language-for-thought.

### Discipline/Method

Neuropsychology (lesion/aphasia studies), neuroimaging (fMRI), developmental psychology, animal cognition

### Key Evidence

**Language is not necessary for thought (aphasia evidence):**
- Individuals with **severe aphasia** (damage to both lexical and syntactic abilities) retain the ability to:
  - Solve math problems
  - Perform executive planning, follow non-verbal instructions
  - Engage in formal logical reasoning, causal reasoning, scientific reasoning
  - Understand others' beliefs (theory of mind), perform pragmatic inference
  - Navigate spatially
  - Make semantic judgments about objects and events
- Researchers carefully verified that the deficit was specifically linguistic (not perceptual or motor)

**Language is not necessary for thought (neuroimaging evidence):**
- In healthy brains, the language network is **"silent"** during:
  - Mathematical reasoning
  - Formal logical reasoning
  - Executive function tasks (working memory, cognitive control)
  - Understanding computer code
  - Theory of mind tasks
  - Semantic judgments about objects/events
- Instead, these tasks engage **other non-overlapping networks** (multiple demand network, theory of mind network)

**Language is not necessary for thought (developmental & cross-species evidence):**
- Pre-verbal infants show inferential and problem-solving abilities
- Deaf children raised without language access can still learn math, relational reasoning, causal chains
- Non-human primates, corvids, elephants, cephalopods exhibit complex problem-solving without language

**Language is not sufficient for thought:**
- Genetic disorders (Down syndrome, Williams syndrome): linguistic abilities largely intact but intellectual disabilities present
- Schizophrenia: affects reasoning, spares language
- Acquired brain damage: some patients have full language but impaired reasoning

### Logic

If language were necessary for thought, aphasia should destroy thinking abilities — it doesn't. If language were sufficient for thought, intact language should guarantee intact reasoning — it doesn't. The double dissociation is the classic neuropsychological argument for functional independence.

### Limitations/Caveats

- Authors acknowledge that **future work might find** some thinking task that engages language areas, but none found so far
- Language deprivation does cause some cognitive delays (but doesn't make complex thought fundamentally impossible)
- The dissociation is already present in young children (ages ~4), which argues against the developmental scaffolding version of language-for-thought

## Block 3: Language is Optimized for Communication

### Claim

The structural properties of human languages — at every level from sounds to syntax — show hallmarks of an **efficient communication code**, not an internal thought system.

### Discipline/Method

Computational linguistics, information theory, cross-linguistic typology

### Key Evidence

**Sounds:**
- Phonemes are spread out in phonetic space → robust to noise, facilitates perception
- Sound repertoires shaped by articulatory ease (physical environment, diet-related anatomy changes)

**Words:**
- Frequent/low-information words tend to be short (Zipf's law generalized)
- Languages re-use short, efficient word forms and sound sequences
- This optimizes retrieval ease and articulatory economy

**Word meanings:**
- Words trade off **complexity** (cost of learning/representing) vs. **informativeness** (precision of meaning)
- Attested systems lie along the Pareto frontier of this trade-off
- Demonstrated for: kinship terms, color terms, season words, grammatical number markers
- Lexical systems also adapt to specific communicative needs of communities

**Syntax:**
- Languages **minimize dependency lengths** cross-linguistically (Fig. 2b,c): verified in 37+ languages
  - Longer dependencies = harder to produce and comprehend (measured behaviorally and with brain imaging)
  - Observed dependency lengths fall below random baseline
- SOV order (most common, ~47% of languages) appears to be the cognitive default (used spontaneously in gesture)
- SVO order (~41%) can be explained by communicative advantage: positional cues help reconstruct who-did-what-to-whom under information loss

**Ambiguity:**
- Chomsky argued ambiguity = evidence against communication function
- Authors counter: ambiguity is **communicatively useful** — allows re-use of short forms, speakers can omit information listeners already know from context
- A language without ambiguity would need much longer words/sentences (example: Lojban, designed to be unambiguous, proved unlearnable)

### Logic

If language evolved for internal thought, these communicative optimizations are hard to explain — our thoughts aren't ambiguous, we don't need to minimize dependency lengths for internal processing, and the trade-off between complexity and informativeness is a communication-specific pressure. These properties make sense only if language evolved under pressure to be easy to produce, understand, and learn.

### Limitations/Caveats

- Authors acknowledge some properties might have non-communicative explanations (e.g., compositionality might reflect pre-existing compositionality in thought)
- But even that concession argues against language-for-thought (directionality is reversed: thought → language, not language → thought)

## Block 4: Evolutionary Evidence — Parallel Expansion of Multiple Networks

### Claim

Brain evolution shows that multiple cognitive networks (not just language) expanded in humans, suggesting language didn't drive cognitive sophistication — they co-evolved in parallel.

### Discipline/Method

Comparative neuroanatomy, evolutionary neuroscience

### Key Evidence

- **Association cortex** (above and beyond perception/motor) expanded disproportionately in human brains relative to other primates
- This cortex contains **multiple large-scale networks**, each supporting different functions:
  - **Language network**: communication
  - **Multiple demand network**: executive function, novel problem solving, math, logic, computer code
  - **Theory of mind network**: social reasoning, mentalizing
  - **Default network**: episodic projection, spatial cognition
- At least some of these networks have homologues in non-human animal brains
- Multiple networks expanded over human evolution, associated with increases in diverse cognitive abilities

### Logic

If language were the substrate for thought, we'd expect a single-factor explanation: language evolved → thought followed. Instead, the evidence shows parallel expansion of functionally distinct networks. The language network is "just one of" many expanded networks, and it is sharply distinct from networks supporting reasoning.

### Limitations/Caveats

- Whether expansion was truly parallel or one network drove others is **not known**
- Authors argue parallel is "more plausible" given that diverse abilities all improved survival probability

## Counter-arguments & Responses

| Objection | Paper's response |
|---|---|
| Ambiguity shows language isn't designed for communication (Chomsky) | Ambiguity is **communicatively useful**: allows re-use of short forms, context-dependent efficiency. A non-ambiguous language would be unlearnable. |
| Language scaffolds development (Vygotsky, others) | Dissociation between language and thought networks is already present in children age ~4. Deaf children without language access still develop complex reasoning. |
| Parsimony favors a single explanation (language → everything) | Brain evidence shows parallel expansion of multiple networks, not a single driver. Parsimony doesn't trump the data. |
| Language facilitates some cognitive tasks | Authors concede this (Box 1), but distinguish "facilitates" from "is necessary for." Words/symbols can compress information, but this compression need not be specifically linguistic (e.g., "9" works as well as "nine"). |
